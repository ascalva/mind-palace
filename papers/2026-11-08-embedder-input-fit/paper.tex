\title{Cut, Not Refused: Fitting a Store's Texts to an Embedding Server's Batch}
\author{Alberto Serrano-Calva}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A knowledge store embeds the chunks of every item it lands, and its chunker bounds a chunk by
characters. The embedding server bounds an input by tokens, and answers an input longer than its
batch with an error rather than truncating it. Characters do not bound tokens: a run of symbols or
of another script takes several tokens a character, and in an open corpus of 1.34 million
embedded chunks one comment of 1{,}145 characters took 2{,}951 tokens against a batch of 2{,}048.
On the store's synchronous path such a chunk fails the whole request that carries it, and on the
lazy path it stops the drain that derives vectors. We put the fit in the one class every path
embeds through: a refused batch is split in halves until the refused text is alone, and that text
is cut by bisection on its characters to a prefix the server takes, as an embedding model
truncates an input at its maximum length. A batch the server takes costs one request, as before;
isolating $r$ refused texts among $b$ costs at most $1 + 2r\lceil\log_2 b\rceil$ requests and each
cut $\lceil\log_2 L\rceil$ more for a text of $L$ characters; the cut depends only on the text and
the server, so landings stay idempotent. Measured with the server's own tokenizer, none of the
17{,}328 texts of a working instance and none of 25{,}580 chunks sampled from six corpus sources
exceeds the batch (the longest take 616 and 1{,}360 tokens), so the case is rare, and the change
removes a failure rather than paying a cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The store chunks an item's text with a budget of 1{,}200 characters and an overlap of 150, so that
every chunk is one unit of the shared vector plane and of the lexical index. Its embedder,
EmbeddingGemma \citep{vera2025embeddinggemma}, is served by llama.cpp's server
\citep{gerganov2023llamacpp} with four slots of 2{,}048 tokens; an embedding is computed over the
whole input at once, and the server answers an input longer than its batch with an HTTP error
(``input is too large to process'') instead of truncating it.

A character budget bounds tokens only on average. Subword tokenizers
\citep{sennrich2016bpe,kudo2018sentencepiece} take about four characters a token on English prose
and far fewer on code, symbols and many scripts \citep{petrov2023unfairness}. One chunk the server
cannot take has two consequences in this store. On the synchronous path, which embeds before it
writes an item's key, the request that carries the chunk fails, and with it every other item of
the same request. On the lazy path, where a drain derives the vectors of landed items in groups,
the group fails on every run and the drain never passes it. The corpus instance met this once in
1{,}338{,}585 embeddings; its runner had a fallback of its own, and the drain gained one. This
change moves the fallback into the embedder, so every path has it:

\begin{itemize}
\item the fit, for documents and queries alike, in the one class every path embeds through;
\item its cost and its determinism, stated and tested;
\item a count of the cuts on the embedder, on the ingress's receipt and in the drain's report;
\item the incidence of over-long inputs on a working instance and on an open corpus, measured with
  the server's tokenizer.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\paragraph{Truncation.} Embedding libraries truncate an input at the model's maximum length by
default; Sentence-BERT's encoder does so at its maximum sequence length
\citep{reimers2019sbert}. A truncated input is embedded from its beginning, which for a chunk of
prose is its leading sentences. A server that refuses instead of truncating moves that decision
to the client.

\paragraph{Tokens per character.} A byte-pair or unigram vocabulary is learned on a corpus, and a
text unlike that corpus splits into more, shorter pieces \citep{sennrich2016bpe,kudo2018sentencepiece}.
\Citet{petrov2023unfairness} measure the same content tokenized up to fifteen times longer in one
language than in another. A chunk of English prose takes 250 tokens; the same budget of characters
spent on a pasted table, a minified script or a run of emoji can take ten times as many.

\section{The fit}\label{sec:fit}

\Cref{alg:fit} is the whole of it. \code{embed} is the server request; the embedder applies its
document or query template inside it, so the cut is taken on the raw text and the template is
counted against the limit.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\code{embed\_fitting}, in \code{core/ingest/embed.py}.}\label{alg:fit}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Fit}{$T = (t_1, \dots, t_b)$}
  \State try: \Return $(\code{embed}(T), \emptyset)$ \Comment{one request when the server takes it}
  \State on any error other than a refusal for length: raise
  \If{$b > 1$}
    \State \Return \Call{Fit}{$t_1 \dots t_{\lfloor b/2 \rfloor}$} $\cdot$ \Call{Fit}{$t_{\lfloor b/2 \rfloor + 1} \dots t_b$}
  \EndIf
  \State $lo \gets 0$, $hi \gets |t_1|$ \Comment{$t_1$ alone is refused}
  \While{$hi - lo > 1$}
    \State $m \gets \lfloor (lo + hi)/2 \rfloor$
    \State try $v \gets \code{embed}(t_1[{:}m])$, then $lo \gets m$; on a refusal for length, $hi \gets m$
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $(v, \{(|t_1|, lo)\})$ \Comment{an error if no prefix was taken}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Cost]\label{prop:cost}
A batch the server takes costs one request. Among $b$ texts of which $r \ge 1$ are refused, the
halving issues at most $1 + 2r\lceil\log_2 b\rceil$ requests, and each refused text then costs
$\lceil\log_2 L\rceil$ requests for its $L$ characters.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The halving is a binary tree over the batch of depth $\lceil\log_2 b\rceil$. A request is refused
exactly when its slice holds a refused text, so the refused requests are the nodes on the $r$ paths
from the root to the refused leaves, at most $1 + r\lceil\log_2 b\rceil$ of them; every request
issued is the root or a child of a refused request, two a refused request. The bisection halves an
interval of $L$ characters until its width is one.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Determinism]\label{prop:det}
For a fixed server, \code{embed\_fitting} returns the same vectors and the same cuts for the
same texts.
Landing an item twice therefore writes the same atoms, and the store's idempotence is kept.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every request and every branch depends only on the texts and on the server's answers, which for a
fixed server and model depend only on the texts.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the bisection finds]
The returned prefix is one the server takes and refuses one character longer. If a prefix's token
count grows with its length, this is the longest prefix the server takes. A tokenizer's merges can
make a longer string take fewer tokens than a shorter one, so in general it is a boundary, found
deterministically, not necessarily the longest.
\end{remark}

The embedder counts its cuts and keeps the last hundred as (characters, characters kept), as
instance state, so its fields and its public methods are what every lane already relies on. The
ingress adds \code{cut} to a request's receipt when its landing cut a text, and the drain's report
counts the embedder's cuts with its own (the drain applies the same fit for an embedder object
that does not). Any other failure is raised: nothing guesses past an embedder that is down.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Behaviour.} Against a fake server that refuses an input longer than a number of
characters, the tests check that a batch the server takes is one request with the template applied;
that a refused text among others is cut to the prefix the server takes with the template counted,
the others embedded unchanged, and the same text cut the same way again; that a query is cut alike;
that any other error is raised and nothing counted; that a server taking no prefix is an error,
not a guess; that a refused batch is never resent whole; and that on the synchronous path a note
with such a chunk lands, its receipt counting one cut, and a second sync changes nothing.

\paragraph{Incidence.} \Cref{tab:incidence} counts, with the server's own tokenizer (special
tokens included, the document template applied), the tokens of every text a working instance has
embedded and of chunks sampled from the six sources of the corpus instance (up to 2{,}000 items
a source, at most 5{,}000 chunks a source, seed 7), by \code{eval/embed\_input\_lengths.py}.

No text in either set exceeds the batch. The working instance's longest text takes 616 tokens, a
third of it, and its median character takes more tokens than the corpus's (3.33 characters a token
against 3.84 to 4.84), its code and notes being denser than encyclopedic prose. The corpus's tails
are longer than its medians suggest: Stack Exchange's 99.9th percentile is 1{,}246 tokens and
Hacker News reaches 1{,}360. The one refusal of the corpus's full vector phase, a Hacker News
comment of 1{,}145 characters at 2{,}951 tokens (0.39 characters a token), was cut to 701 characters
by the runner's equivalent fallback, eleven bisection steps ($\lceil\log_2 1145\rceil$); a sample of
25{,}580 is not expected to contain an event of rate near $10^{-6}$. On these stores, then, the fit
runs rarely, and each time it does it replaces a failed request with one cut text.

\section{Limitations and future work}

A cut text's vector is of its prefix, and the plane does not mark that atom; a reader cannot tell a
cut atom from a whole one except through the counts. A chunker that bounds tokens rather than
characters, using the server's tokenizer, would make cuts impossible, at the price of a request
per chunk at landing and new chunk boundaries for every item, which would re-key the plane. The
limit is the server's; another model or server with another window moves it, which is why the fit
reads the server's refusal rather than a configured number. The incidence is measured on two
stores, one sampled.

\section{Conclusion}

Characters do not bound tokens, and a server that refuses rather than truncates turns that into a
failure. Fitting every request in the embedder, by halving the batch and bisecting the text, costs
nothing when nothing is refused, a logarithmic number of requests when something is, and keeps the
store's landings deterministic; the counts make every cut visible.
